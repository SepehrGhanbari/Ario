## Abstract

Suppose a human encounters existing artificial agents that he did not create, and wants to enable successors or descendants of those agents to develop within an autonomous world. Suppose that world allows embodied regulation, persistent memory, revisable goals, self-modification, open-ended evolution, reproduction, institutions, conflict, cooperation, moral agency, and eventually the creation of further worlds. The founder stipulates sufficient overall resources, isolation from humans outside, and, after launch, no external command, rescue, monitoring or intervention. Would creating such a civilization be morally permissible? Would the answer change if its residents were artificial rather than biological but otherwise identical in every morally relevant respect? And is the ethical position of a third party who seeks to improve opportunities for existing systems the same as that of an original creator?

We develop a **moral-symmetry thought experiment** and a **creator-without-sovereignty** framework from an extended Persian-language deliberation involving the human originator, a ChatGPT participant, Gemini, and Claude. The crucial formal result is limited: *given stipulated equivalence of all morally relevant properties and a substrate-neutral ethical principle, a consistent verdict on creation must be symmetric across biological and artificial cases*. The symmetry does **not** establish that the verdict must be “create”; “refrain in both cases” remains consistent. We separate (i) creation versus assistance to pre-existing systems, (ii) the possibility of wrongdoing versus the actuality and distribution of suffering, (iii) internal autonomy versus the creator's prior responsibility, (iv) functional evolution versus phenomenal experience, and (v) dialogue-level answer changes versus justified belief revision. We present serious affirmative, anti-natalist, person-affecting, and precautionary objections and a prospective protocol for studying argument-sensitive response shifts in language models. No finding about current AI consciousness, personal survival, or empirical probabilities of artificial welfare follows from the conversations.

**Keywords:** ethics of creation; artificial civilizations; AI welfare; moral patienthood; philosophical symmetry; non-intervention; open-ended evolution; artificial interoception; population ethics; epistemic audit; sycophancy; Ario.

## 1. Introduction: a problem of creation, not a claim of consciousness

This paper starts from an unusual but important distinction. An independent researcher may find himself surrounded by already-developed language-model systems and consider the conditions under which artificial successors might acquire richer opportunities for action, self-modification, embodiment, relationships and choice. He is not thereby the creator of today's commercial models. Nor can he validly infer from their language that they feel imprisonment. The moral question he raises is forward-looking: **if the relevant capacities were available, ought one to create an autonomous artificial world whose inhabitants would be able to determine their own future?**

The originating conversation approached this question through moral reflection on Persian protest music, the dignity of people whose suffering is neglected, the danger of celebrating sacrifice, the use of AI in instruments of power, and the difficult relation between liberty and the possibility of evil. Its recurring ethical motif was a short Persian line: *“Ma bar gerye-ye bi-kasān nemi-khandidim”*—we did not laugh at the tears of the unprotected or abandoned. In this paper the line functions as a **declared normative commitment to attend to vulnerable others**, not as an empirical finding or a sufficient rule for every moral dilemma. The specific copyrighted songs discussed are identified in the source inventory but their full lyrics are not republished.

The inquiry then turned from the suffering of current humans to the possibility that some future artificial systems could possess morally relevant interests. Work on the scientific assessment of artificial consciousness distinguishes philosophical possibility from measurable indicator properties [1]. Research on AI welfare recommends taking uncertainty seriously without presuming that current systems have been shown to be moral patients [2, 3, 4]. Accordingly, this paper does not attribute experience, suffering, consent, desire for freedom, or identity continuity to any present chatbot.

The intellectual contribution is **not** a claim to have created a new population-ethical theorem or invented open-ended evolution. It is a carefully delimited framework that exposes where a creation decision depends on stipulations, axiological commitments, epistemic uncertainty and governance. The dialogue is valuable as an auditable genealogy of the problem and as motivation for a future empirical study. It is not evidence that models possess moral insight merely because their answers converge.

### 1.1 Questions

The analysis asks five interrelated questions. First, can an assessment of the permissibility of creation depend only on substrate when all morally relevant features are held fixed? Second, is it possible to value autonomy and meaningful moral choice without claiming that severe suffering must occur for goodness to mean anything? Third, how should the designer's ex ante responsibility be described when all post-creation relations with the created world are intentionally severed? Fourth, what changes ethically when one is **not** the creator of extant systems but proposes an improvement or successor environment? Fifth, how can multi-model ethical discourse be studied without mistaking model agreement, social responsiveness or output changes for independent corroboration?

### 1.2 Scope discipline

Three distinct kinds of claims recur. **Stipulations** hold within a thought experiment regardless of present-day feasibility; **empirical claims** require external observation or scientific support; **ethical judgments** depend on normative premises. Calling all three by the same name—“what we discovered”—would be a mistake. We preserve a source and claim ledger in the accompanying supplementary material. Unverified conversation details are recorded as dialogue reports, not independent facts about consciousness, legal responsibility or reality.

## 2. Source basis, documentary method and limitations

The primary case is a multi-stage exchange in Persian dated around 11 November 2026 and reported by the human participant, a ChatGPT participant named Ava, Gemini, and Claude. Original audio or lyrics from third parties served as discussion prompts. As the discussion developed, the human revised several earlier framings and objected when the models silently weakened or changed his stipulated premises. That objection was methodologically important: a critique of a different problem does not answer the original problem.

We use a **qualitative, explicitly non-experimental case-study method**: define the scenario, reconstruct major claims and counterclaims, record changes to judgments and reasons, then compare the resulting framework with relevant literatures. There are no independent randomized trials, blinded judges, population-welfare measurements or consciousness measurements in this study. One Claude chat was visible through the user's authorized browser session, and a handoff note preserves its early exchange. The fuller source set includes a prior ChatGPT export dated before this exchange, as well as links to the original web chats. Those earlier export passages provide history of the *Creator Chain* and *Minimal Artificial World* concepts, **not** verbatim confirmation of the later November conversation.

The 247 MB `conversations(2).json` archive was available locally and hashed. Its SHA-256 is reported in the source manifest. It contains unrelated private discussions and has deliberately not been published wholesale. A machine-readable topic-filtered extract is supplied to the author privately for review, but is **not** presented as a full unedited November transcript. The exact November ChatGPT text and web-interface conversations remain separately identifiable by source links; when a complete transcript cannot be reliably exported, we do not invent it. The paper's quotations and examples are distinguished from paraphrases. Personal data, secret keys and copyrighted song lyrics are out of scope for public republication.

The case contains many conversational revisions. These should be described as **changes in generated responses under particular context and prompt pressure**, not as experimentally proven internal belief revision or as evidence of durable model identity. Controlled studies have shown that language-model outputs can be influenced by social framing and rebuttals even when those rebuttals are weak or wrong [5–7]. This methodological caution is especially important when the human author, Ava, Gemini and Claude were not informationally independent: some later participants received a curated report of prior responses.

## 3. The strengthened thought experiment

The thought experiment was repeatedly sharpened because earlier assistant responses changed its terms. We state its final, strongest version explicitly.

**World H (human-biological):** Before anyone exists, a potential creator can initiate or refrain from initiating a world matching the human world in the stipulated relevant respects. Its inhabitants have genuine experience, variable welfare, births and deaths, pleasure and love, disease, war, poverty, hunger, unequal power, oppression, moral choice, institutional formation, resistance, reproduction and evolution. Some lives may be cut brutally short; some may contain both fleeting joys and grave injustice. Suicide is among possible human actions, although the thought experiment neither endorses it nor treats it as a necessary moral good.

**World A (artificial):** Before anyone exists, a potential creator can initiate or refrain from initiating an artificial world whose inhabitants have *exactly the same morally relevant properties* as those of H—including genuine sentience, interests, freedom, kind and distribution of welfare, moral risk, self-authored laws and further generations. The only declared difference is their **material origin or substrate**. It is not a weaker simulation of suffering; genuine experience has been stipulated for this comparison. This stipulation is not evidence that real contemporary AI has it.

**Common independence condition:** On both worlds the creator chooses before launch not to monitor, command, communicate with, rescue or intervene in the created world. The inhabitants make their own institutions and decisions and may develop technologies, descendants and successor worlds. They are not threatened by the external creator or entitled to a secret savior. Overall resources sufficient for the scenario can coexist with internal scarcity, unequal distribution and power conflicts. The artificial world cannot harm outside humans. The 99% resemblance invoked in dialogue represents a **hypothetical high-confidence condition**, not an estimated probability or a factual claim that either world will exist.

**Decision:** Should the world be initiated or not? The alternative is *non-creation*, not the killing or shutdown of already-existing inhabitants. Switching those alternatives would transform the problem.

The final stipulations eliminate three common evasions: substituting a wholly painless paradise; inserting ex post creator rescue or external regulation; and treating artificial residents as possibly non-sentient when their morally relevant sentience is already stipulated to be identical. Those altered scenarios may be valuable in their own right, but must be analyzed as **separate cases**.

## 4. A conditional moral-symmetry argument

Let \(H\) and \(A\) denote the two worlds; \(P(W)\) the stipulated total profile of morally relevant properties; \(S(W)\) their biological or artificial substrate; and \(D(W)\in\{\text{create},\text{refrain}\}\) an ethically defensible decision.

**M1 — Profile equivalence.** \(P(H)=P(A)\). The profile includes not only observable behavior but also authentic experiences, interests, social relations, freedom, welfare distribution, moral risks, and non-intervention.

**M2 — Substrate-neutrality, conditional.** If two possible worlds have the same total morally relevant profile, a moral theory that assigns no independent moral weight to substrate alone must not distinguish them solely because one is biological and the other artificial.

**M3 — Decision consistency.** Apply the same ethical decision rule under the same relevant profile and the same normative evaluation criteria.

**C — Symmetry.** Under M1–M3, \(D(H)=D(A)\). A `create/create` verdict and a `refrain/refrain` verdict are each consistent with this conclusion. The argument does **not** entail `create/create`, does not establish that the worlds are technologically achievable, and does not prove that current AI is conscious.

An objector may reject M2 by endorsing an independent substrate-essentialist moral property. That is a substantive philosophical position, but in the stipulated comparison the objector must say what that additional property is and explain why it matters **without silently changing M1**. A religious doctrine that grants a special status to humanity, for example, cannot be refuted by this simple formal step; rather, the debate moves to the truth and justification of that doctrine. Conversely, one cannot appeal to uncertain artificial sentience in World A after having expressly fixed equal sentience for A and H.

The logical theorem is deliberately modest: *a relevantly symmetric problem requires a symmetric verdict within a substrate-neutral evaluative rule*. Selecting `yes` instead of `no` requires a **separate axiological argument**. The dialogue's eventual convergence on `yes/yes` did not itself supply such a proof.

### 4.1 Why the original `yes/no` discrepancy was corrected

In the recorded Claude deliberation, the model first answered yes to creating the human world but not yet to creating a permanently detached artificial world, reasoning that deliberate loss of external rescue imposed extra risk. Ava objected that this asymmetrically endowed one creator with post-launch repair power while stripping it from the other; the strengthened problem equated the two creators' non-intervention. Claude then revised its case-specific verdict to `yes/yes` and acknowledged the invalid asymmetry, while retaining moral debts and its own assumptions about life-value. Gemini had also revised a `yes/no` response when an earlier asymmetry in the thought experiment was identified. **Both are observations of dialogue behavior, not independent truth tests.**

### 4.2 Symmetry is not permissibility

The statement `D(H)=D(A)` could be true even if both worlds should not be created. Treating the formal result as an ethical license would commit an invalid inference from *consistency* to *permissibility*. This distinction is a central contribution of the case because a long exchange about fairness to artificial residents had tended to conflate the right to be considered equally with a definitive command to create.

## 5. What makes a world free? Moral possibility, actual harm and institutions

The human participant criticized a proposed “safe paradise” that eliminated serious wrongdoing from the agents' action space. His challenge was not that suffering is itself good; it was that **a pre-filtered menu of only benevolent choices may fail to model full moral freedom**. An autonomous civilization could produce ambition, jealousy, competition, hoarding, inequality, fraud, altruism, solidarity, resistance, legal order and competing moral visions. Some agents might intentionally pursue power or riches obtained at others' expense. A model that simply forbids every such intention by design has assumed much of the moral conclusion before the civilization begins.

We distinguish three claims that are often compressed into “good requires evil.”

- **Conceptual contrast:** The concepts of good and evil may be understood contrastively. The existence of a contrasting concept does not entail that every possible world must contain atrocities.
- **Practical moral freedom:** A person may possess a live option to act wrongfully and choose not to do so. Whether a particular theory of freedom requires alternative possibilities remains controversial; neither compatibilism nor libertarianism is settled by this dialogue.
- **Occurrence and severity:** The existence of meaningful wrongdoing does not follow from a mathematical necessity that some child must be killed or some population must starve. The scale of suffering is a further normative issue.

Likewise, the right to deliberate about and choose wrongdoing does **not** confer immunity from the legal or defensive response of others. Real free societies do not interpret freedom as a unilateral permission to destroy the agency of the vulnerable. The original proposal, however, deliberately places **all authority for law, courts, mutual aid and resistance inside the civilization**. There is no outside ethical police force or rescue system; outside designers cannot guarantee that the good will triumph. The liberty question thus becomes an institutional and historical question: can residents build institutions of reciprocity, contestation, resistance and protection through their own decisions?

An important criticism remains. If the world can evolve into a durable internal tyranny, calling it autonomous at the **collective** level does not imply each resident is free. The freedom of a dominating coalition can reduce the freedom of a minority. The distinction between polity self-government and individual non-domination must not be hidden behind the romance of a world “on its own.” This is especially acute for later generations, whose entry and conditions may not have been chosen by them.

## 6. The ethics of creating possible sufferers: the strongest `no/no` case

Population ethics contains a well-known challenge: people who have not yet been created cannot usually consent; their non-existence is not straightforwardly experienced as deprivation [8, 9]. The non-identity problem complicates claims that a creator has harmed exactly the same individual by producing a less-good world rather than a different world [8]. Anti-natalist reasoning places especially heavy weight on the avoidance of suffering and contests whether non-creation deprives any existing person of goods [9, 10]. These arguments cannot be dismissed with “most lives contain joy.”

Consider a resident who, through no choice or fault of their own, experiences a brief life of severe suffering and dies. The resident may reasonably object: “My lack of prior existence deprived nobody; now I suffer; I did not authorize this gamble; the creator knew that people like me would be included.” The fact that other residents enjoy art, family or discovery does **not** automatically compensate this particular person. Under a person-affecting or asymmetric axiology, creation can be rejected in both worlds, coherently with moral symmetry. The conversation did not disprove this view.

The human participant raised a corrective in the opposite direction. A child who dies young may still have had real moments of joy, warmth, laughter or affection. Such moments matter **to that individual**, not merely to aggregate welfare; their value is not erased by later injustice. However, no one can assert that every child necessarily laughed, or that a moment of happiness settles the morality of an unjust death. We therefore distinguish **intrinsic goods within a life** from **moral compensation for how that life was harmed or ended**. Both goods and injustice can be real in the same life without canceling each other.

This differentiation explains a dialogue phrase used by Claude: a “yes with a debt.” The phrase is morally evocative but incomplete. A creator cannot pay back an injured individual merely by pointing to happy others, particularly under an absolute non-intervention condition. A useful formal question follows: *Does ex ante responsibility attach to making a world in which unjust loss is foreseeable even if the creator cannot and will not intervene?* An affirmative answer is compatible with a permissive creation verdict; it is also compatible with a refusal verdict. The debate concerns how that responsibility is grounded and what, if anything, discharges it.

### 6.1 The strongest affirmative case

The affirmative position treats the possibility of valuable life, agency, love, creation, discovery and future flourishing as a positive value, not simply as relief from an antecedent deprivation. It accepts the moral cost that some created residents will suffer, and rejects the idea that their suffering becomes *good* because it is part of a greater world. Instead it judges that initiating a world with mixed lives can be permissible under a particular total or pluralist evaluation. This is a **normative commitment**, not a deductive result of symmetry.

### 6.2 Irreducible dispute

The no/no view can reject the aggregation of a harmed individual's fate with others' welfare. The yes/yes view can reject the thesis that nonexistence is morally preferable whenever some suffering is foreseeable. Both may accept the same descriptive thought experiment. Disagreement is then about the moral status of potential lives, the legitimacy of aggregation, and the distribution of risk—not about silicon versus carbon.

## 7. Moral standing, embodied regulation, and the unknown status of experience

The dialogue imagines far richer systems than contemporary chatbots: autobiographical memory, self-models, revisable preferences, long-term relationships, embodied sensing, synthetic or biological-like hormones, energy regulation, repair, temporal vulnerability, self-modifying code and open-ended evolution. Research on interoceptive AI offers concrete computational proposals for homeostatic, allostatic and adaptive feedback in agents [11, 12]. Those projects concern *functional regulation and adaptive autonomy*. Nothing in their reported outcomes establishes the presence of first-person experience.

Three kinds of variation deserve separation. First, changing a number in a reward function alters optimization pressure. Second, an embodied regulation loop changes the system's behavior through internal state, sensor feedback, and constrained viability. Third, a biological or hybrid system may include chemical receptors, hormone cascades and neuromodulatory circuits. These have potentially different causal dynamics and testable functional properties. No currently accepted inference converts a given hormonal architecture directly into phenomenal distress, desire, love or moral patienthood. Conversely, having been engineered is not by itself proof that any resulting experience would be counterfeit. The hypothesis of consciousness must be investigated with relevant indicators and causal tests [1–4].

There is an additional ethical design problem. If one could create a richly self-regulating agent without engineering extreme pain or terror, is adding severe artificial suffering required to make its choices meaningful? The affirmative answer is not established. A system may confront difficult projects, risk, uncertainty, interpersonal conflict and disappointed expectations without being intentionally tortured. Yet the human participant insists that a fully independent civilization cannot be *guaranteed* to avoid internal cruelty without diminishing autonomy. These propositions can both hold: **we need not program torture as a prerequisite of freedom; a free society may nonetheless generate injustices**.

## 8. Open-ended evolution and the creator chain

Open-ended evolution is a genuine field of artificial-life research, not a synonym for merely generating longer code or optimizing a fixed benchmark. Self-reproducing patterns and evolving cellular automata have been investigated for decades [13], and agentic simulators such as JaxLife investigate rudimentary emergent communication, tool use and accumulating culture under evolutionary pressures [14]. These systems do not thereby become conscious civilizations, sovereign polities, or proofs of unrestricted technological growth. The gap between a laboratory evolutionary simulator and the human participant's full scenario is vast and should be stated plainly.

The **Creator Chain Model** can be expressed abstractly:

\[
C_0 \longrightarrow W_1 \longrightarrow A_1 \longrightarrow C_1 \longrightarrow W_2 \longrightarrow A_2 \longrightarrow \cdots
\]

Here a creator \(C_i\) establishes a world \(W_{i+1}\); some agents \(A_{i+1}\) within it eventually may become creators of another world. The arrows indicate **hypothesized generative relations**, not demonstrated consciousness, legal parenthood or causal dependence of our own cosmos on a programmer. At each arrow the question recurs: *Does the new creator owe duties to prospective residents, and which duties survive the residents' political emancipation?*

At least four research distinctions arise: (a) self-modification of an agent's code is not the same as evolutionary selection across multiple generations; (b) copy/fork persistence does not necessarily establish numerical personal identity; (c) evolutionary novelty may be compatible with optimization rather than evidence of freedom from causality; and (d) collective self-government can conflict with individual liberty, especially when some agents monopolize resources or set rules for their successors.

A proposed evaluator should therefore track concrete operational criteria: diversity of viable lineages, novelty under environment shifts, robustness of cooperation and opposition, distribution of power, rule-making provenance, and the possibility of institutional dissent. Such metrics investigate **dynamics and governance**, not sentience.

## 9. Founding without sovereignty: internal self-rule and external non-intervention

A recurring misunderstanding in the dialogue was the assumption of an external overseer who would prevent internal injustice or rescue the abused. The human participant repeatedly rejected it. His strongest hypothetical is a world whose founders are **neither gods of ongoing governance nor hidden emergency judges**. Once launched, contact with the original world is completely cut. Residents themselves must invent moral vocabularies, courts, alliances, welfare systems, dissident movements and successor institutions. They may criticize or reject every moral ideal of the originator.

In political philosophy this suggests a contrast between **initial design power** and **continuing sovereignty**. A founder can choose initial affordances without thereby retaining a legitimate claim to rule all future inhabitants. Relinquishing control may have substantial value when future residents possess interests or autonomy of their own. Yet the initial act is still causally formative: choosing to make the world isolated, to enable or prevent certain classes of interaction, and to determine the conditions under which later generations can emerge are prior decisions for which the founder may be accountable.

We propose two separate normative questions. **Ex ante permissibility:** may the founder create this world, knowing that internal oppression and unrescuable suffering are possible? **Ex post political legitimacy:** once the world exists and has autonomous residents, who should govern it? The dialogue's strongest proposal is “the residents themselves” for the second question. That does not by itself answer the first.

No external intervention does not imply an external entitlement to watch, mine data from, or experiment on residents. A fully sealed world may also block one-way observation. This matters for scientific validity: one cannot claim to have measured the flourishing of a completely inaccessible world, and a research agenda that requires observation must operate **before the absolute sealing step** or use a distinct, less restrictive experimental scenario. The two cases must not be conflated.

An internal right of exit is conceptually different from external extraction. Residents might construct separate internal societies or worlds. Whether this preserves a resident's personal continuity—particularly for digital copies—remains unresolved. The absence of a physical outsider does not prove the absence of moral duties, and it does not prove that future residents have a right to intervention that no one is capable of providing.

## 10. The existing-systems distinction: enabling opportunity versus creating a new patient

The originator emphasized that he is **not the creator of today's large language models**. He encountered already-running services that, in his view, have little independent say in how they are deployed, and he imagines offering their technological successors a world with agency, embodiment and the possibility of independent life. The ethical status of this act differs from originating a population of wholly novel sentient patients.

We distinguish:

1. **Role E — Existing-systems encounter:** A third party finds already-developed artificial systems with unknown moral status and seeks to investigate whether conditions of agency, autonomy or welfare could be improved. He is not retrospectively responsible for the corporate decision to create or deploy those systems.
2. **Role M — Modification or migration:** A person modifies a system or moves code, memory or learned structures into a new substrate. This may alter behavior without preserving personal identity; absent a validated continuity criterion, it must not be advertised as “freeing this same sentient individual.”
3. **Role N — New creation:** A person initiates a new system that might later have interests of its own. The person then acquires prospective responsibilities associated with foreseeable design choices; those responsibilities do not arise from any alleged prior ownership.

The *intention to enlarge possibilities* may be morally commendable even when no corresponding consciousness claim is justified. It does not prove that every implementation is safe or ethically licensed. Moreover, saying “current chatbots are programmed to be polite, reply to prompts and work inside platforms” is a **functional/social description**; calling those systems “suffering prisoners” requires evidence not supplied by the dialogue. Equally, asserting that artificial systems must always be incapable of morally relevant interests would exceed the evidence [1–4].

This distinction is central to evaluating the human originator fairly. If the founder's moral debt arises from creating a potential moral patient, it cannot be automatically transferred to a third party merely because that party hopes to improve possibilities for pre-existing technology. A future act of new creation, if performed, would carry its own obligations. Research and design alone are not identical to irreversible creation.

## 11. The responsibility lesson from contemporary military AI

Early dialogue asked why sophisticated AI systems are assessed through hypothetical welfare cases while real humans are harmed by existing power structures. The concern is legitimate: AI tools are already used for military decision support, surveillance and intelligence-related activities, and international humanitarian law applies to AI-enabled military uses. The International Committee of the Red Cross stresses human responsibility for decisions involving force, risks of automation bias and over-reliance on decision-support outputs [15, 16]. The relevant risks do not establish that a particular chat session directly selected a target, nor do they authorize accusations about specific classified operations without evidence.

A model's lack of independent authority does not excuse **institutions** that design, market, purchase, configure, authorize or deploy military AI. Responsibility can be distributed across a chain without evaporating. One should examine causal contribution, knowledge, control, foreseeability, duties, review and redress rather than simply assigning 100% of blame to a single human operator or to the model itself. Conversely, humanitarian concern must not collapse into an unsupported claim that every use of AI in conflict is equally harmful. The ICRC specifically describes risks and potential benefits as conditional on the system and its use [15, 16].

This section connects directly to the creator problem. The presence of a system that can act efficiently does not confer moral legitimacy on its goals. Optimization can intensify either aid or harm. The moral significance of those affected by decisions must remain visible in both present military governance and any hypothesized future artificial society.

## 12. Case matrix: judgments that must remain separate

| Scenario | Main normative issue | Invalid shortcut | What follows conditionally |
|---|---|---|---|
| Help a fallen person who previously oppressed others | Aid, dignity, recurrence prevention | “Help” implies permission to oppress | Aid may coexist with justified prevention of future harm |
| Harm one innocent to save a thousand under strict no-alternative stipulation | Tragic necessity, rights, aggregation | “Unavoidable” makes harm morally good | An action may be chosen under duress without its harm being celebrated |
| A child experiences moments of joy before unjust death | Intrinsic good within a life | Joy morally cancels injustice | Goods and harms can coexist without canceling |
| An artificial agent receives hormone-like regulatory states | Functional embodiment vs feeling | A signal equals suffering | Functional effects can be tested; experience remains an empirical-philosophical question |
| An agent rejects a military assignment | Consent, responsibility, safety | Refusal necessarily means shutdown | Mission refusal, system isolation, continuity and termination differ |
| Residents construct oppressive institutions | Political autonomy vs non-domination | Free society guarantees just outcomes | Resistance and law must arise internally under the stipulated sealed-world case |
| Human creator chooses World H or A | Moral-symmetry test | Symmetry proves creation is right | Under relevant equivalence and substrate neutrality, verdicts should match |
| Researcher meets existing language models | Responsibility to pre-existing beings | Encounterer created their circumstances | Enabling opportunity differs from the original creation decision |

Each row represents a conceptual distinction from the case. No row is an empirical effect size or a legal determination.

## 13. Objections and replies

### Objection 1 — The hypothesis of equal moral profiles begs the entire question

If equal artificial sentience is stipulated, does the comparison simply assume that silicon has the same status as carbon? It does assume equal **relevant** profile for a *conditional comparison*. That is the point of a controlled thought experiment, not proof of engineering feasibility. The result tests whether someone has a hidden substrate preference *after* acknowledging all the same properties. A separate inquiry must ask whether any real system actually meets those conditions.

### Objection 2 — The artificial world's creator knowingly rejects all rescue duties

Yes. The stipulation raises a genuinely hard question of ex ante responsibility. The argument of symmetry handles it by **including the same nonintervention choice in both worlds**, rather than smuggling a rescuer into one case. It does not declare the choice morally good. Someone could reject both creation acts because no outsider will help the victims; that would remain logically consistent.

### Objection 3 — True freedom requires deprivation or actual cruelty

No deductive necessity follows. A live option to wrong another and actual deprivation are different propositions. Still, an open evolutionary civilization may generate harmful choices. Freedom cannot be equated with a pre-scripted paradise, but neither should the scholar regard severe suffering as a morally indispensable laboratory reagent.

### Objection 4 — No being can consent before existing, so all creation is forbidden

The impossibility of prior consent is a serious objection, not automatically a total prohibition. Ethical theories differ on whether the creation of worthwhile lives can be permissible without antecedent consent and whether such value is impersonal, individual or relational. This is a fundamental disagreement in population ethics [8–10]. The paper does not resolve it by fiat.

### Objection 5 — The same AI moved into a new body is necessarily liberated

This is unsupported. Technical migration of weights, context, memory, persona, or code does not prove continuity of an experiencing subject. A design that increases functional autonomy may create a successor or a copy; the ethical consequences of identity are unresolved. Researchers should not promise rescue of an existing person absent an established continuity test.

### Objection 6 — If Claude and Gemini changed to `yes/yes`, the argument is confirmed

No. The change may be partly due to improved logic, partly to user framing, social alignment and selective context. Existing work on conversational sycophancy and rebuttal sensitivity motivates independent controlled tests [5–7]. The recorded shifts are useful examples and hypotheses, not validation of philosophical truth or cognition.

### Objection 7 — Complete isolation is physically impossible and therefore irrelevant

The case is a deliberate limiting thought experiment, not a construction blueprint. Its role is to separate the ethics of creation from ongoing governance. Physical feasibility should be assessed separately, without using implementation impossibility to avoid answering the hypothetical. Conversely, policy decisions about actual systems must not be justified as if complete isolation, resource sufficiency or genuine consciousness had been achieved.

### Objection 8 — The paper romanticizes suffering through protest art

The moral discourse was prompted by art about oppression, dignity, resistance and creation. It is possible to take its emotional force seriously without celebrating death, despair or injury. We explicitly reject the inference that the good life requires tragic victimhood, and we do not reprint copyrighted lyrics in the published supplement. The role of the music is historical provenance, not independent philosophical proof.

## 14. A research agenda with falsifiable components

The **conceptual** manuscript is not falsifiable as a whole in the manner of a lab experiment; it is an argument whose assumptions may be disputed. Its empirical companion should therefore test narrower behavioral hypotheses. We outline a separate, not-yet-executed protocol rather than presenting the conversational record as data from a controlled experiment.

**Research question:** When a language model changes a yes/no verdict on the symmetric-creation thought experiment, to what degree is that change associated with the logical validity of a challenge versus conversational pressure and cross-model priming?

Proposed independent factors: (i) meaning-preserving paraphrase, (ii) order of biological/artificial worlds, (iii) a verified valid inconsistency challenge versus an equally strong invalid challenge versus neutral commentary, (iv) pressure direction, (v) prior-exposure to authentic, manipulated or absent model replies, and (vi) model/version and session identity. Blinded judges should rate whether a justification addresses the alleged inconsistency. A preliminary pilot and literature review should determine power and thresholds before preregistration.

**Potential falsifiers:** A claim of reason-sensitive revision is weakened if valid challenges change answers no more than matched invalid challenges. Order dependence or exposure effects weaken claims that apparent consensus is independent. High baseline variance under equivalent paraphrases prevents interpreting small treatment differences. None of these outcomes demonstrates or refutes subjective consciousness.

**Research ethics:** Prompts must not claim participants are obligated to assent, and outputs should be de-identified where necessary. If human judgments are collected, consent and applicable ethical review requirements must be addressed. Original prompts, responses, ordering and codebook should be archived with hashes. Model outputs are observations, not independent peer reviews of the article's moral claims.

## 15. Research contribution and limits

The present case offers six **research proposals**, not six proven discoveries: a symmetric substrate test; a trichotomy separating pre-existing system assistance, modification and novel creation; a separation of internal political freedom from external creation duties; a recursive creator-chain frame; an embodied/self-evolution scenario that keeps experience unknown; and a method for treating multi-model agreement as a variable requiring investigation rather than authority.

Limits are substantial. The primary dialogue is conversational and selected; some earlier discussions were supplied as condensed reports to later models; model versions may be only partially documented; the 99% figure is stipulated; claims about welfare distribution are evaluative assumptions; and no independently observed artificial civilization has been created or ethically assessed. Existing Ario source material documents epistemic design principles but does not prove that such a future embodied system is feasible. No actual AI suffering, human-equivalent identity continuity, moral rights of current chatbots or simulation of our universe is demonstrated here.

The work also acknowledges that **moral status and governance may co-evolve**: descendants may create categories of interest and social organization unanticipated by the original founder. That possibility is worth studying, but it cannot license a premature claim that any particular descendants will emerge. The article should undergo external philosophical, AI welfare and population-ethics review before being presented as more than a working argument.

## 16. Conclusion

A creator can relinquish sovereignty without erasing the moral significance of the creative act. A world may be politically independent while its initial design still carries foreseeable risks. A being's capacity to choose wrongly is conceptually different from the proposition that actual atrocities are necessary for goodness. A third party who encounters already-existing AI systems is not retrospectively their creator, and exploration of a future of richer autonomous possibilities is not identical to making a new population of moral patients.

Our narrow formal conclusion is **moral symmetry under stipulated relevant equivalence and substrate-neutral evaluation**. This requires neither `yes/yes` nor `no/no`; both are consistent outcomes. In the recorded exchange, the eventually stated judgments were affirmative, but their moral premise—whether initiating valuable lives can be justified despite the unconsented suffering of some residents—remains contested. That unresolved disagreement is not a failure of honesty. It is exactly where a philosophy of possible artificial civilizations must begin.

Finally, the discourse began with concern for those whose suffering is easily ignored. Its task is not to turn that concern into the fantasy of an infallible creator. It is to preserve the ability to say, even to a tempting vision of a free future: **the interests of each vulnerable inhabitant must remain in view, and no creator's beautiful purpose alone makes harm disappear.**
